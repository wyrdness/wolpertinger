## Overview

The Wolpertinger is a folkloric beast of the alpine forests of Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg in southern Germany. It is conventionally depicted as a chimera with the head of a hare or rabbit, the body of a squirrel, the antlers of a deer or roebuck, the wings (and sometimes legs) of a pheasant, and prominent fangs, though the exact mix varies between specimens. The Wolpertinger is closely allied to other European hybrid creatures — the Thuringian Rasselbock, the Swedish Skvader, the Palatinate Elwedritsch, the Austrian Raurackl — and to the American Jackalope. It exists primarily as folklore and as a Bavarian taxidermy tradition: 19th-century Bavarian taxidermists assembled fanciful 'Wolpertinger' mounts from parts of multiple animals as inn decoration and tourist amusement, and these mounts remain staples of mountain-region inns and souvenir shops. The Deutsches Jagd- und Fischereimuseum (German Hunting and Fishing Museum) in Munich maintains a permanent display of Wolpertinger specimens. Some 17th-century European woodcuts of horned hares are now thought to depict real rabbits afflicted with the Shope papilloma virus, which produces horn-like growths and may have helped seed the Wolpertinger and Jackalope traditions.

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Shope papilloma virus seed** _(Modern zoologists and folklorists)_

Some 17th-century European images of horned hares may depict real rabbits infected with the Shope papilloma virus, which produces horn- or antler-like keratinous growths from the head — a plausible biological seed for the antlered-hare motif behind the Wolpertinger and Jackalope.

### Skeptical

**Hoax taxidermy and tourist humour** _(General folklorist consensus)_

The Wolpertinger is openly understood as a 'gaff' creature: a chimera assembled by Bavarian taxidermists for inn decoration and tourist humour. Its status as folklore is uncontroversial.
